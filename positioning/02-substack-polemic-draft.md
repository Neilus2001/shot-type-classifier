So let's talk about the difference between filmmakers and tech, and about where the rest of us have to stand.

## Filmmakers are craftspeople

A filmmaker's instinct is to make the thing well. You obsess over a cut you know only nine people will notice. You argue about a lens. You rewrite a scene at 2 a.m. because the silence in the middle of it is wrong.

That's craft, and it's sacred. I'm not writing this to tell anyone to stop.

Craft has one blind spot, though. It assumes the work speaks for itself. It assumes that if the film is good enough, the world will find it and pay for it.

The world does not work that way. It never has.

## Tech is systems building

Tech people don't obsess over the lens. They obsess over the pipe: who touches the thing, where the money moves, who owns the relationship with the person at the end.

A good engineer doesn't ask "Is this beautiful?" They ask "Does it scale? Does it repeat? Who controls it?"

That's why every decade, tech builds the system that filmmakers end up living inside. And every decade, filmmakers are told this one will be fair.

## The history of the promise

- **Home video** was going to let independent films reach everyone.
- **Digital cameras** were going to democratise production. (They did. They also flooded the market.)
- **Crowdfunding** was going to let the audience fund the art directly.
- **Streaming** was going to be the great equaliser: infinite shelf space, global reach, a fair licence fee. [Your insider observation on what actually happened to licence fees, residuals, or the vanishing mid-budget deal.]
- **And now AI** is being sold as the tool that finally frees the filmmaker.

Each promise was real for a little while. Each one got captured by whoever owned the system. None of them were villains. They were systems, and systems pay their owners first.

## Creators stand at the center

Somewhere between the craftsperson and the system builder, a new kind of person showed up: the creator.

Creators get sneered at in film circles. I get it. "Content" is the word that flattened cinema into inventory.

But look at what a creator actually is: someone who makes the work *and* owns the relationship with the audience. No distributor in between. No platform deciding the licence fee. The creator is a craftsperson who accidentally built a system.

## The three doors

Every filmmaker now stands in front of three doors.

**Door one: just make films.** Commit to the craft completely and let someone else handle the system. There's nothing wrong with this. Most of the films I love were made by people who chose this door. Choose it with your eyes open, though. You're betting that whoever owns the system will treat you fairly. Look at the history above and decide how much you're willing to bet.

**Door two: build systems.** Own your audience, your data, your distribution, or at least one of those. It's slower. It takes time away from the craft. It asks you to become, at least partly, the thing film school taught you to look down on. It's also the only door where nobody else can change your terms.

**Door three: wait for someone to build a fair system for you.** A system that pays a living wage and lets you just make movies. It might come. Maybe a union, a co-op, or a platform with a conscience. But you've heard this promise before. You heard it with streaming.

I'm not telling you which door to choose. I'm telling you that *not choosing is choosing door three.*

## Why I'm calling this Legacy of Folktales

Folktales are the oldest distribution system in the world, and they never had a distributor.

No studio owned them. No platform took a cut. They survived for centuries because the teller owned the relationship with the listener. Every telling was a small act of distribution, and every generation updated the story for the room it was in.

That's the model I'm building on. **Legacy of Folktales** is my bet on door two: make work with the care of a craftsperson and own the fire it's told around.
